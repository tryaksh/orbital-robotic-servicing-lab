What does a robot need in order to service a machine in orbit?

A six-axis arm captures a modular compute unit, pulls it out of a rack, and puts one back — in vacuum, in freefall, holding the module by real contact rather than a simulation shortcut. This is the measured answer to what interface that takes, and what it costs to do it from a camera instead of from perfect knowledge.

The result

The same trained policies, the same physics, the same code path. The interface

The hardware matters more than the intelligence

The first attempt gripped a smooth post with flat pads. It held 6 N — against the 66.4 N that the insertion's own contact reaction demands. No amount of training fixes that; it is a statement about geometry. Redesigning the module-side feature against the gripper's measured collision envelope, rather than its nominal dimensions, changed the answer by an order of magnitude.

Flat pads on a post6 Nfriction only
Required66.4 Nmeasured insertion reaction
Head-on grapple pin69 Ngeometry on the load axis

Getting there needed one non-obvious idea. A tapered pin converts closing force into thrust along the very axis the module has to leave by, so gripping harder pushes the payload away. Capture and hold had to become two separate commands — close gently at 0.48 rad, firm to 0.68 once loaded. The window is narrow and asymmetric: 0.44 holds 63 N, 0.52 holds 68 N, 0.56 collapses to 26 N.

Zero gravity is not just "gravity off"

Three mechanisms that only exist in freefall

  • You cannot grasp a free-floating mass by squeezing it. Any asymmetry in contact timing becomes net momentum, and nothing absorbs it. Measured: the gripper launched a 10 kg module 32 mm before the jaws closed. Terrestrial grasping quietly relies on the object resting on something.
  • A wedge is a thruster. Closing force becomes payload thrust along the pull axis. Raising grip force from ~100 N to the gripper's rated 235 N made holding capacity worse, not better.
  • Capture has to happen while the rack still holds the module, so the rails absorb the closing asymmetry — and only then is it broken free.

Industrialisation

How well does the camera have to be mounted?

The first question anyone building this asks. A pose estimator trained through a perfectly mounted camera demands a perfectly mounted camera — a 2 mm mount error quadruples its error. Retraining across eight deliberately mis-mounted cameras loosens that requirement about five-fold.

Estimates falling inside the 20 mm capture tolerance. Both fail beyond the randomized envelope, which is left visible.

And the trade has a sting worth reporting. The mount-robust estimator is better on every calibration and worse in the loop — 38.19% workflow success against 80.38%. Its held-out error is 2.51 mm, barely worse than 1.75 mm, but its time-averaged bias is 9.15 mm against 5.88 mm, and bias is the part no filtering removes. An offline perception metric did not predict closed-loop success.

The other half of the record

What was tried and did not work

Five hypotheses were tested and refuted with measurements. They are kept because they are what justified the decisions that followed, and because a project that reports only its wins is not reporting.

An anti-yaw yoke on the pinrefuted Cost insertion 67 points to buy extraction 0.13. Then the rotation was finally decomposed: it is split evenly across two axes and the walls opposed only one.
A modelled capture latchrefuted Swept 10–160 N·m against an unchanged policy: moved the target rotation by 0.006 rad and collapsed extraction travel from 458 mm to 25 mm, by jamming the module in its rails.
Penalising contact forcerefuted Two penalty strengths, the stronger charging the same order as the success reward, moved mean contact by 2.6%. Force control needs force feedback.
Force sensing for pose robustnessrefuted The force-aware policy was worse beyond its trained range. Cause found: the rack's lead-in flares were already doing the alignment mechanically.
Blaming the workcell geometryrefuted Converged IK reaches the extraction pose holding attitude to 0.0114 rad, 17× inside tolerance. The earlier evidence against it was an under-converged solver.

Two probes were also caught measuring nothing at all — a yaw gate that reported an identical number with and without the feature it tested, because the rails held the module still; and a camera-shake probe whose camera moved by exactly 0.0 mm. Both were deleted along with the conclusions they had produced. The rule that caught the second one was written after the first.

Method

Why the numbers are worth reading

  • Promotion gates, not screenshots. A policy is promoted only on a pooled multi-seed report with zero instability and zero non-finite terminal metrics.
  • Quoted results are cryptographically tied to the checkpoint that produced them. A tool compares the SHA-256 of every loaded policy against the evidence files; it caught a chain quietly running a superseded policy the night it was written.
  • Each phase of the chain is given the clock its own skill was certified on, derived from the task rather than restated, so "it works in the chain" and "it scores X alone" cannot be different claims.
  • Negative and retracted results stay in the record, including two published claims that were withdrawn after re-measurement.

Simulation only. No result here was produced on real hardware, and nothing on this page is a flight-readiness claim. Single training seed per policy; evaluation seeds are held out.
